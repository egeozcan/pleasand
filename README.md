# pleasand

A falling sand game, with peasants in it. The valley is four screens wide, and you look
along it: swipe sideways, Shift-wheel, the arrow keys, or drag the strip of the whole
valley under the field.

Twenty-odd kinds of pixel (sand, water, salt water, oil, wood, plant, fire,
lava, stone, metal, ice, snow, gunpowder, ash, salt, seed, glass, acid, gas,
torch, faucet, drain, cloner, wet concrete, and inert: a magic block that stays where you put
it, carries no heat and ignores everything but the eraser), plus fireworks, a
nuke and lightning. And under the sand, in the rock: seams of coal, lumps of iron ore, beds of rock salt and,
deep down, the odd speck of gold, and here and there a pool of oil sealed in the stone, waiting for somebody
to drill for it. MORE, under ERASE, opens onto the rest you can paint: that rock (coal, iron ore, gold, rock
salt) and the peasants' building stone (stonework, concrete, firebrick, stained glass).

Every pixel has a temperature. Heat flows from hot to cold, fast through metal
and slowly through wood, ash and air; hot air and hot water rise, cold air sinks.
Heat is never made or lost on the way: it only gets out (or in) at the edges of
the room, so a fire leaves the air warm until its plume has found the top. Only
burning things, bangs, lightning, torches and the HEAT brush add any. Things melt,
boil, freeze, set and catch fire at their own temperatures, and soak up (or
give back) latent heat while they do it, so a pond takes a while to boil and
lava stays runny under its crust. Ice melts, water boils, steam rains (or
snows, if you chill it hard), stone runs as lava past 1150 and lava sets again
below 950, sand sinters into glass, glass and metal melt and set, wood, oil,
plants and gas catch on their own when hot enough, and salt water boils down
to salt. Coal catches late and burns a long while; iron ore runs, and what
runs out of it sets as iron. Also a small village of
peasants who walk about, chat, climb things, jump ledges and gaps, carry ninja ropes and parachutes (which mostly open), swim, catch fire, run for
the pond, go to space if thrown hard enough, and have opinions about all of
it. On ice they take a while to get going and a good deal longer to stop:
turn round in a hurry, or run, and down they go, sliding along on their
back until the ice runs out, or a wall comes, or somebody else's legs do
(thrown onto a frozen pond, they do not stop either). They have names. The
game keeps count. They grow old, too, and in the end die of old age, quietly, in bed or
sitting about (or, if nobody ever feeds them, of hunger) (and get a cross, once somebody has thought of crosses); and a
valley with room in it gets somebody new walking in from the edge now and then (the valley keeps up the count it
started with, plus any you make yourself). There are ducks, too: they
waddle, swim, dabble, fly, eat seeds, push through bushes and flowers, and
sometimes bring ducklings. Now and then another duck waddles in from the edge, unless the valley is full of them.

The peasants build. They chop wood, saw each log into four boards, and put up
things you cannot paint yourself: **houses** (on a levelled plot, a plank floor,
a door at each end that only peasants can open (water and sand cannot, though
the odd duck slips through behind them), a roof
with a window that lights up when somebody is home, and a bed), **doors** and
beds for the two old huts, **stairs** or a **ladder** up any wall too tall to
jump, and **bridges** over gaps too wide to leap and ponds too wide to bother
swimming. Anybody with wood helps with anybody's project; what is still to come
shows as a faint blueprint. Then they live in it: they sleep in their beds (or
on the ground, and complain), run home and hide when things go bang, go and
stand by a fire when they are cold, light campfires and gather round them,
climb ladders, find their way round (up the stairs, over the bridge, down
the ladder, up a rope onto a ledge, through a tree, up through it onto the top
of it or down through it) to wherever they are going, go exploring
when they are bored (somewhere high, somewhere nobody has been lately), put back what you burn down (and mourn what you burn right
down), and plant saplings where they cut trees down, which grow into trees.

A peasant who is really hungry drops everything (the mine, the building
stone in their arms) and goes for the ripe wheat in the nearest field,
however far off it is.

And when they have eaten well, they think. Every tick a peasant spends well
fed, and not frightened, freezing or asleep, goes towards a bright idea (twice
as fast at home, round a campfire, holding a fishing rod or standing about by
lamplight). When it comes, a
light bulb goes on over their head, the village hears about it, and from then
on they all know it. Something has to put an idea into somebody's head first:
nobody thinks of buckets until something has gone up in flames. And it is
whoever it happened to who thinks of it: the one who struck the ore thinks of
smelting, the one watching the fire thinks of buckets, the one who went for
wood and found none thinks of forestry. Otherwise, it is mostly whatever has
been on everybody's minds longest. Hover over IDEAS
at the top to see what they know, and what might give them the rest. What they
think of:

- **Farming**: a field of sand tilled into dark furrows and sown with wheat,
  which grows (faster with water about: it drinks what lands on it), ripens
  from the ear down, and is eaten, all of it, after which the field is sown
  again. It takes no wood, so anybody lends a hand. A village with fields is
  a well-fed village, and a well-fed village has more ideas
- **Lanterns**: one on a bracket by each door, burning in a warm pool of light
- **The bucket**: once something has burned (or been set alight by somebody
  bored), or once they know about digging and have a field to keep watered, a fire at anything
  of theirs sends everybody who was only doing something for fun to the pond
  with a bucket (on the near side of the fire: they are brave, not daft). On
  a quiet day they water the wheat. The alarm wakes everybody in a burning
  house and sends them out of the nearer door (not the one with the fire in
  front of it), and nobody runs home to a house that is on fire. A peasant who
  is burning runs for the pond from further off, or for the nearest bucket,
  and anybody with a full bucket runs at them and tips it over their head. On a
  bridge with water under it they just drop through the planks into it
- **Landscaping**: comes of turning earth over (hoeing a field, shovelling out a
  plot). With that and the bucket, a house or field with no water within forty
  cells or so gets a pond of its own: a bowl dug into the sand beside it (it
  takes no wood, so anybody lends a hand), the banks dug back a cell at a time
  so the sand stands, then filled bucket by bucket from the nearest fresh water.
  The brigade finds it like any other water, and somebody tops it up when it
  runs low (from real water: never out of another pond, or two ponds would
  keep the brigade carrying the same water back and forth for ever). Water
  sealed in a cave under the house is no use to a bucket, and salt water no
  use to wheat, so neither counts as water nearby. They need level-ish sand to
  do it, so a pond only goes in where the ground allows (and a pond dug where
  nobody can get to it to finish it is given up, and the next one goes in
  somewhere else).
- **Tidying**: once they know landscaping, the rest of the valley starts to
  look untidy. A puddle lying about the village (a spilt bucket, a melted
  snowman) is bailed out and carried to a pond that is short of water, or a
  field that is thirsty. Water on somebody's floor, on the bridge or at the
  foot of the tower is in the way: all of it is bailed, however much was spilt
  (they paddle in it to get the last of it, filling the bucket as they go),
  and goes in whatever real water is a short walk off, or else out of the door
  and over the nearest bush, which can do with a drink (somewhere lower than
  the floor, so it does not run back in). Whoever lives there sees to their
  own floor, and their own roof, before the graves, the mine or the rest of
  the valley's tidying. A drop or two is left to dry, one bucket goes to a
  puddle, nobody throws a bucket through a wall (it only comes back down on
  the floor), and a puddle in a field, a pond or a mine is that place's own
  business. Salt water is left well alone. Sand that has come down on
  something they built (a roof, the thatch on the old huts, a bridge deck, the
  stairs, the lookout), or drifted up against a door so nobody can get in, is
  swept off (or dug away) a handful at a time and carried off an armful at a
  time until the roof is clean, which is worth mentioning. Ash and salt off it
  go in a pocket, sand too if they make glass, and the rest is tipped into a
  hole in the ground somewhere, which it fills in (they are pleased with
  themselves when it comes out level: a crater, or a pond they gave up on), or
  else on the village heap, on flat ground well away from anybody's house,
  which grows. The heap is the village's own: nobody builds on it, sows a
  field over it or plants a tree in it, and once it is a proper heap somebody
  sticks a stick in the top of it, with a rag tied on, so everybody knows.
  A pile of sand tipped out in the village where the ground was level (out of
  the sky, by a blast, by somebody's dropped armful) is shovelled up and
  carried off the same way, a couple of armfuls at a time, until the ground
  is as it was; the lie of the land (a dune, the spoil by a mine, a hollow
  filled flush) is let be. A bush that has grown up in somebody's doorway is
  cut back, a snip at a time (a door, not a hedge), and let grow a good while
  before anybody looks at it again. Something more urgent comes up, and the
  armful goes down where they stand once they are clear of the house, not into
  thin air.
  A roof or a door that a dune keeps burying is let be a while once it is
  swept, and a house or a roof nobody can get to, after a couple of tries, a
  good while: the village says so
- **Gravestones**: once somebody has died, a wooden cross for each of the
  dead, in a row, saying how. Now and then somebody visits, and leaves a flower (or, with gold to spare,
  a nugget of it: the statues get those too). One graveyard, not a cross on every hill: it holds ten at
  most, and when it is full (or there is no room left near it) the oldest cross has weathered away,
  and the new one goes where it stood
- **Forestry**: comes of going for wood and finding not a tree left standing.
  After that, whenever the woods about them run thin, somebody plants a
  sapling where it has room to grow (a crown's width from the next tree, and
  well away from anybody's house), and in time there is wood again
- **Fishing**: a jetty out over the pond, and somebody on the end of it with a
  rod. A fish, mostly. Sometimes a boot, or a duck. If you boiled the pond,
  the fish come out cooked
- **The scarecrow**: the ducks like the wheat too (they eat the bottom of the
  stalk, and the rest comes down as seed). Not with one of these about. Until
  then, anybody who knows farming and is only doing something for fun runs at a
  duck in the wheat, arms going, until it takes off (ducklings are left alone,
  and a fire comes first)
- **Fireworks**: once they have a couple of houses and a couple of ideas, now
  and then somebody puts on a show (well away from anything that burns), and
  everybody comes to watch. Coal out of anybody's pockets makes for more
  rockets, a nugget of gold for a golden finale, and a barrel of oil from a
  well's tank for a bigger show

And under the sand there is rock. What comes up out of it gives them the
next idea, and the next:

- **Mining**: comes of digging enough holes (or standing on bare rock and
  wondering). A mine: five columns dug down through the sand, the sand
  either side held back by a collar of timbers, a shaft three wide straight
  down into the rock with a ladder in it, row by row, so whoever is at the
  bottom can always climb out, and the spoil tipped on a heap beside it. Then
  galleries off the bottom, pushed out a stretch at a time, towards whatever
  is worth having (they look a good way along the rock), following a seam up
  into the roof and down into the floor. Timber props every few steps (iron
  ones, a bar a prop, once there is a forge and iron to spare), the
  rock dark, and a warm pool of candlelight round every miner. A pick takes a
  cell at a time, and what comes out goes in their pockets: stone, coal, iron
  ore, gold. The first of each is news, and where nothing has been found yet is
  worth going a long way to dig. (When there is nowhere at all left in the
  valley for a mine, they dig up a field for it, if they have one to
  spare (two more besides, one of them close by) or somewhere else to sow it, and sow it again there if they can.) A mine takes no wood, just a pick:
  anybody lends a hand. When the bottom of it is worked out both ways, a deep
  enough shaft gets an upper level, halfway up, and that is worked out too;
  then they sink another. (The spoil heap is kept a good way from the shaft,
  low, and well away from where anybody stands to work a furnace, a pump or a
  tower's door, so it does not slide in over them. If sand gets in anyway,
  the site is reopened and it is dug out; if it keeps sliding back as fast as
  they clear it, they leave it for a good while. A site nobody can get to at
  all, after enough tries, is given up.)
- **Masonry**: comes of a heap of stone. A watchtower, seven wide and two dozen
  high: a door at the foot of each side, so it is in nobody's way, a ladder
  up the inside, built a row at a time with the walls round it so whoever is
  building it climbs what they have built, and a lookout on top, jutting out
  on corbels, with battlements and a lantern. The bored go up and look out
  over the valley (and shout if they see smoke), and the explorers love it.
  And with stone to spare, a new house goes up in stone, whatever burns
  out of an old one is put back in stone, and the old wooden houses are done
  over in stone, a house at a time, which does not burn (the doors, the
  window and the bed are still wood, and the old boards go in somebody's
  pocket)
- **Smelting**: comes of finding both coal and iron ore (and coal is good for
  a campfire, too, when there are no logs: it smoulders a long while). A furnace by the
  mine, with a mouth and a chimney. Everybody's ore and coal goes in it, and
  whoever is there works the bellows: the hearth glows, the chimney smokes,
  and out comes a bar of iron. And with iron, iron picks: mining goes
  nearly twice as fast
- **The forge**: comes of iron to spare. Whoever has no tools goes to the
  furnace and beats a bar of iron into an axe, a hoe and a hammer: chopping,
  hoeing, digging and building all go faster. They wear out, a stroke at a
  time, and then it is back to the furnace: the worn heads are good for half a
  bar of scrap, and the rest has to come out of the mine
- **Street lamps**: comes of a forge, lanterns, and a good deal of iron lying
  about. An iron post six tall (half a bar a section) with a lantern on top,
  on level ground between the houses, clear of the doors and the fields: one
  at a time, a dozen paces apart, up to three for every house. Nobody has to
  walk round one (they step through ironwork), and the street glows at night
- **The lift**: comes of iron, and a long ladder. An iron headframe over the
  top of a mine shaft or a tower, a wheel, a chain, and a cage, which comes
  to whoever wants it and runs up and down four times as fast as anybody
  climbs. They work out their routes by it, too
- **The bell**: comes of gold, and a tower to hang it from (or, with no tower,
  the beam of a lift, or the eaves of a house). A golden bell on a bracket out over the battlements. It rings for every new idea, and when
  anything of theirs is on fire it rings and rings, and the whole valley
  hears it: the sleepers wake, and the bucket brigade comes from however far
- **The statue**: comes of gold to spare, and of somebody who has had three
  bright ideas or more. A likeness of whoever has had the most, in dressed
  stone on a plinth, one arm up, with a head of gold and a light bulb over it
  that never goes out. The bored stand in front of it and think (twice as
  fast), say what its subject thought of, and, if they are its subject,
  admire it. Later, gold and stone to spare, the next greatest mind gets one
  too (three, at most), whether they are still about or not
- **The oil well**: comes of a smell of oil under the rock (a mine whose
  workings come near a pool of it will smell it) and of iron enough for a derrick. An iron deck,
  a frame of iron legs and braces, and a ladder up the inside to build it by. Somebody
  works the pump at the foot of it, and every stroke brings up a barrel (the
  bore can be turned, down in the rock, towards oil that is off to one side). The
  first few go in the tank; after that the well overflows, and the oil pools at
  the foot of the derrick, black and patient and extremely flammable. The oil is
  finite: when the pool under it is empty the well runs dry, and they sink
  another. (With nowhere else for a derrick, they dig up one of their fields
  for it, if they have one to spare or somewhere else to sow it.) (Do not bring a torch.)
- **Rocket packs**: comes of a tank of oil and a bar of iron, and somebody with
  more nerve than sense. A peasant puts one together at the well: a tank, a
  nozzle, a seat, and no brakes. Then, when they are bored enough, they light it
  and go up (pointed at the valley, not the edge of the world; with a roof or a
  tree over them, they walk out to somewhere with open sky first). They come down
  again, mostly. A full pack is good for two flights; after that it is back to
  the well to fill it up from the tank, while the oil lasts. The ducks are not
  impressed

And then there is what is left over: the ash of every fire, the salt a pond of
brine boils down to, the lumps of glass the lightning leaves in the sand, snow,
ice, the odd pinch of gunpowder somebody left lying about, and (once they
smelt) any little lump of metal, which is scrap iron. They pick it all up (it
might come in handy: hover over a peasant to see what is in their pockets), and the mine has beds of **rock salt** in it now, an old sea dried out,
which comes up as salt. Some of it is just for fun:

- **Snow**: with snow on the ground and nothing much to do, somebody scoops up a
  snowball and lets fly at whoever is nearest. Whoever it hits throws one back
  (it is cold, and down their neck), and anybody standing about joins in. Or
  they build a **snowman**: rolled out of the snow about, a cell at a time from
  the bottom, with coal for eyes if anybody has any, a carrot, a scarf, a hat,
  twigs for arms, and a name. He lasts as long as the cold does, and when he
  melts, somebody is sorry
- **Ice**: is for skating on. On purpose. Back and forth, until the turn at the
  end where it goes wrong
- **Frost**: wheat out in the open grows nothing below freezing, and a hard
  frost kills it from the top down (try the COOL brush on a field)

And some of it puts ideas into their heads:

- **Potash**: comes of sweeping up ash by a field. Ash raked into the furrows:
  the wheat comes up twice as fast for a good while, and the earth goes grey
  with it
- **Salt**: comes of salt in a pocket and a fall on the ice (or a village that
  fishes). Salt is flung on the ice where anybody went over, and by every door,
  and the ice turns to brine. And a fish caught on a full stomach is salted and
  kept, and eaten later, when they are hungry, instead of a long walk to the
  field
- **Black powder**: comes of finding coal, salt and ash, all three (or a pinch
  of the real thing). Ground together in a bowl, carefully, well away from any
  fire. A charge at the face of a gallery, a fuse, FIRE IN THE HOLE, and
  everybody back down the gallery: four columns of rock out at once, and what
  was in them in the pockets of whoever lit it. A charge thrown in the pond
  brings up fish for everybody on the bank (the ducks leave). A charge or two
  in a fireworks show makes a bigger show. And a peasant on fire with black
  powder in their pockets has a short, eventful afternoon
- **Glassmaking**: comes of a lump of lightning glass, a frost on the wheat, or
  a furnace that has been hot a good long while. Sand and coal at the furnace
  come out as panes of glass, and a field gets a **glasshouse**: a glass roof on
  iron posts (which they walk through). Under it the ground's warmth stays in
  (none made, none lost: it comes up out of the earth, instead of going up into
  the sky): the wheat grows faster, needs no watering, and keeps growing through
  a cold snap a good while after the open fields have stopped. A long hard
  freeze gets in in the end, and a quick chill with COOL stops it under there
  as it does anywhere
- **The store**: once there is a furnace, pockets full of ash or salt are
  emptied onto its pile, and whoever needs some for the bowl or the field
  fetches it back out. With the bucket and salt both known, a village running
  short of salt fetches a bucket of brine from any salt water about and boils
  it down at the furnace: steam up the chimney, salt in the pan. And with salt
  in a pocket, a pinch of it goes on a meal, which then goes further

Building with stone (or iron, or gold) works as building with wood does,
except that a site keeps a pile: whoever brings some puts it on the pile, and
whoever is building builds from it, so somebody with stone and somebody with
wood can build a stone tower with wooden doors between them. Hover over a
site to see what it is waiting for.

And when there is not much left to think of, they build something nobody needs
and everybody wants. The great works are ideas too (hover over **WONDERS** at
the top to see which they have built, which is going up, and what might give
them the rest), put into somebody's head by something that happened to them,
and each comes with a banner. One goes up at a time, with a crew of a third of
the village at most, so nobody starves for it; whoever has what it is short of
brings it from anywhere in the valley. A wonder that cannot get what it needs
for a long while is laid by, and the next one is started. Two plain ideas come
first, because the great works need them:

- **Concrete**: comes of masonry, smelting and the bucket, in whoever spills a
  bucket at the furnace. Stone and coal burnt together at the furnace come out
  as lime, and lime, sand and a bucket of water, mixed, come out as a pail of
  wet concrete, which is carried, poured, and sets in a while, like stone (twice
  as fast warm; frozen, it never sets, and crumbles). Somebody who dawdles with
  a full pail ends up tipping out a lump of concrete wherever they are. The
  furnace also fires stone and coal into firebrick, which stands far more heat
  than stone does. You can pour **WET CONCRETE** yourself: hurry
- **Stained glass**: comes of glassmaking and ore found, in whoever makes the
  next pane. A pane, with a pinch of ore in it, comes out green; with coal,
  amber; with a fifth of a nugget of gold, ruby. It glows its colour when there
  is a light near it

And a great work wants a great deal of stone, which is in nobody's pocket for
long. When one is short of it, a miner goes down an old mine and cuts the floor
of a gallery down, a bench at a time, and brings the stone up; with no mine,
the bare rock somewhere. Gold is only ever gilding: a wonder is finished in
plain stone where there is none, and gilded when some turns up. And while a
great work wants iron, new tools wait. What they build:

- **The theatre**: comes of fireworks, masonry, a village of six or more, and
  a few things worth telling. A plank stage two up on a stone plinth, a
  backdrop, red curtains (one hung full at the back, one tied back to the post
  at the front), a painted cloth of a hill and a moon between them, and a
  lantern at either end of the beam. In front of it, three tiers of stone
  seats, cut into a slope if there is one and built up out of stone if there
  is not. Every so often the boredest of them picks the best tale nobody has
  acted yet, two of them (three, in a big village) play it, and the idle sit
  on the tiers and watch, and clap. Whoever it happened to plays themselves if
  they are about, or says from the seats that it was not like that
- **The pyramid**: comes of masonry, somewhere to cut the stone, and a king
  dying; the next to be crowned thinks of it (a valley whose king never dies
  does it for its greatest mind instead). Twenty-three wide at the foot, twelve
  courses of dressed stone, each a step in from the last, so its sides are
  stairs, out at the edge of the valley where it is in nobody's way. In the
  middle, sealed in, a chamber with the king's name and crown. The capstone is
  gold if there is any, stone if there is not, gilded later if some turns up.
  The bored climb it and look out, the new king goes up now and then and says
  something about the old one, and somebody hears about it from outside and
  comes to live in the valley. Open the tomb, and the first one in is cursed:
  for a good while they trip over their own feet, on sand, on anything, and
  say so. And the pyramid is down as wanting mending, like anything of theirs
- **The baths**: comes of concrete, coal, and a good deal of swimming; whoever
  last climbed out of the pond, shivering, thinks of it. A basin of concrete
  sunk into the ground, nine by three, filled bucket by bucket from real water,
  with a firebrick hearth under one end and a flue under the floor to a chimney
  at the other (a hypocaust, the Romans called it), and columns and a
  pediment over it all. A stoker puts lit coal in the hearth, and the heat comes
  up through the floor into the water, the way heat goes anywhere here: under
  33 they stoke, over 40 they rake the fire out, and say so. Anybody cold goes
  and bathes instead of standing round a campfire, and the bored go for a soak
  and gossip about whoever is not there; warm water is cosy, and they think
  twice as fast in it. Over 50 they get out and stand round it shouting at the
  stoker
- **The hanging gardens**: comes of a village that knows concrete looking for
  room for another field and finding none, anywhere. So: up. Three terraces up
  a slope (or on brick piers, where there is no slope), each a little field
  six wide, five rows over the last and open to the sky all the way up, with a
  pool dug at the foot and a cistern at the top. The terraces are fields like
  any other, sown and reaped and raided by ducks, but they do not count
  against the fields a village keeps. Between the pool and the cistern, up
  under the terraces, runs an archimedes screw of iron, and the screw-turner
  stands at the crank and turns it, and sings, and every twenty strokes a cell
  of water comes up out of the pool and into the cistern. The cistern spills
  onto the top terrace, the top terrace onto the next, and the bottom one back
  into the pool: the same water, round and round, less what the wheat drinks
  on the way. The pool is topped up a bucket at a time
- **The steam organ**: comes of concrete, a show or two, and a furnace that
  has been worked hard. A copper tank of forty cells of water over a firebrick
  firebox, and on its lid a rack of seven tinned pipes from six to eighteen
  high, each with a valve where it leaves the lid. Before a show (and now and
  then on its own) the organist fires it, a coal at a time, until there is a
  head of steam under the lid; then opens the valves to a tune, one note at a
  time. The steam goes up whichever pipe is open, and every puff of it out of
  the top of a pipe is that pipe's note. A concert is about thirty notes. It
  plays at every fireworks show. The steam goes up to the top of the sky,
  cools, and comes back down as rain about where it rose: what lands in the
  gutter at the organ's foot the organist bails back into the tank, and the
  rest is rain on whatever is next to it. The audience sways. Somebody always
  says it is a bit loud. Shut every valve on a tank full of steam for long
  enough, and the organist opens one, to let it breathe
- **The duck palace**: comes of the scarecrow, forty raids on the wheat, and a
  valley with ducks in it, in whoever has shooed the most of them. If you
  can't beat them, house them: a plank deck at the edge of the biggest fresh
  water there is, half on the bank and half out over the water, a pavilion
  with a little dome on it (gold, if there is gold to spare; painted, if not),
  three nests, a ramp, and at the bank end a trough. Whoever reaps the wheat
  keeps a handful of seed back, and the feeder scatters it in the trough, and
  is mobbed. A duck will go a long way for seed in the trough, a good deal
  further than for the wheat, so the raids fall off. A duck at home on the
  deck has ducklings twice as often, and they hatch there. The eldest duck
  wears a crown. Throw a duck at it, and the ducks will not go near it for a
  while
- **The ice palace**: there is no weather in the valley, so this one is yours.
  Freeze the village (a snowman, and somebody skating, first), with snow lying
  about the houses, and they build a palace out of it, quick, before it thaws:
  packed-snow walls, a crenellated tower and a gatehouse, ice windows, an ice
  floor, and a lantern hanging from the skylight. Anybody free lends a hand,
  whatever else is going up. They skate in it, they throw snowballs round it,
  and they stand in it out of the cold. The lantern's warmth is real, and the
  skylight drips. When it warms up it melts, and once it is more water than
  palace it is a ruin, and they are sorry. The next time you freeze them, they
  build it again
- **The cathedral**: comes of coloured glass, the bell, and a cross weathered
  away to nothing; whoever was at the graveyard when it went thinks of it. A
  stone nave seventeen wide with a door at either end, pews, three candles
  hung from the vault, a rose window of coloured glass in the gable (green and
  amber, and ruby at the heart if there was gold), a belfry, and a spire
  thirty-five up with a lightning rod and a weathercock on it. The bell is
  taken down from wherever it hung and rehung in the belfry. Under it all is a
  crypt, a ladder down through the floor, with a niche and a candle in the
  wall for every name the village remembers: the ones whose crosses weathered
  away come back. The bored go down with a candle and say hello. Every so
  often the bell rings for a service: the idle file into the pews, and the
  eldest names the dead, and how ("for Gary, who came back from space too
  fast"), and everybody goes home thinking twice as fast for a while. The
  candles light the rose window from behind, the only coloured light in the
  valley. The rod takes a bolt that was going to hit anything of theirs within
  ten columns of it, and only that. Water that gets into the crypt is bailed
  out, a pail at a time, up the ladder and out onto lower ground, or into the
  pond. Far from home when the fireworks go off, it is where they hide
- **The observatory**: comes of glass, masonry, and a good many looks out from
  the watchtower (twenty, all told; it occurs to whoever has looked out most).
  A whitewashed dome on top of the tower, with a slit in its shoulder and a
  brass telescope through the slit (on the highest ground there is, if there
  is no tower). An astronomer goes up now and then and looks: at the moon,
  every time, and at the stars, which they join up into a constellation and
  name (THE BIG DUCK; THE LADLE; sometimes after somebody lately dead). It
  hangs over the valley, faintly, for a while. Every so often they say there
  will be falling stars tonight, and there are, and anybody with nothing
  better to do stands and watches. One shower in three, one of them comes
  down: a lump of iron with a nugget of gold in the middle of it, cold by the
  time it lands, on open ground and never anywhere near anybody's house or
  anybody. Somebody goes and picks it up
- **The lighthouse**: comes of oil, glass, masonry, and a lake: a real one, a
  hundred and fifty cells of water at the least, which no valley has unless
  you give it one. It occurs to the first one to swim in it. A red and white
  tower on the bank, a ladder up the inside, a gallery with iron rails, and a
  glass lantern with two lamps and a gilded reflector. The keeper carries a
  barrel of oil up from the well's tank whenever it is getting low; a barrel
  is a minute or so of light. The beam goes round, and whoever it passes over
  thinks a little faster; a swimmer in trouble swims for it. Somebody out
  there sees it too, the first night, and comes to live in the valley. Let the
  oil run out and it goes dark, and everybody knows whose job that was
- **The foundry**: comes of iron tools, concrete, sixty bars of iron made, and
  whoever has worked the furnace most. A firebrick chamber in a firebrick
  stack, a tap at the foot either side, and a mould sunk under each: a trough
  for plates, and a cup for an engine bell. The founder tips it full of iron
  and coal in layers, works the bellows, and keeps the fire fed (raking the
  ash out as they go, which is more ash than the valley has ever had); when
  most of it is running, they knock the plug through and it runs into the
  mould like water, only brighter. Once it has set and cooled, it comes out as
  plates, a fifth of a bar each. It wants a great deal of coal. Only the moon
  rocket wants plates
- **The moon rocket**: comes of the moon looked at a dozen times, the foundry,
  concrete, a few flights on a rocket pack and a good many barrels pumped; the
  astronomer thinks of it. A concrete pad with a firebrick trench, a red
  girder gantry with a ladder up to a walkway, and a rocket: fifty-odd cast
  plates in a hollow shell, an engine bell under it, a porthole, red fins, a
  gilded nose. It wants twelve barrels of oil, carried a barrel at a time from
  the well. Then a crew of up to three (whoever has flown most on a rocket
  pack, the astronomer, and whoever has had the most ideas) climb the gantry
  and get in, the valley comes to watch from a safe way off, and there is a
  countdown. It goes up on a roaring column of smoke and glare, out of the top
  of the world,
  and a little while later there is a flag on the moon. The crew come back the
  way anybody comes back from space (over the pad, this time; the parachutes
  mostly open), with moon rock in their pockets, which they set out by the
  statue. They wear the helmets for good after that, and will tell you about
  it

Two of them are yours: there is no weather in the valley, and no lake big
enough, unless you make one. The rest a valley gets as it has room for them.

And the valley fills up. One screen of it has level ground for four or five
houses, the two old huts among them, and eight people to put in them: three or
four sleep on the ground all their lives, and say so every morning. Once they
know masonry, whoever wakes up on the ground next, with nowhere left for a
house, thinks of building on top of one instead (`docs/storeys-plan.md` is how):

- **Storeys**: the gable comes off a finished house (the boards go in somebody's
  pocket; the ceiling stays, and nobody downstairs is unroofed), and a flat goes
  up in its place: walls, a window, a roof with a window in it as the house's
  has, a bed, and a door at one end with its own ladder up the outside to it.
  Somebody homeless lives there now. Any house takes one; a stone house with a
  stone flat on it takes a second, its ladder up the other end, so every flat
  has its own way in and its own way out (and nobody climbing to the top floor
  gets off at the middle one). Nothing takes a fourth floor. The neighbours come
  up for a look round when it is done, six of them in a room nine wide, and
  after that they talk through the floor ("do you have to stomp?"). Somebody
  climbing past the window on the way up is seen ("i can see you, you know"). A
  bucket spilt upstairs is bailed, and thrown out of the upstairs door,
  GARDYLOO, onto the ladder, and on whoever is on it. In a fire (in a village that
  knows the bucket) everybody in the stack is woken and makes for the door that is
  not on fire, and nobody climbs down a ladder with the fire at it: they jump
- **Word gets round**: once they build upward, a valley where everybody has a roof
  and nobody has gone without for a good while draws a newcomer now and then
  ("i heard there was room upstairs"), who is homeless, and puts the next flat
  up. It stops when the roofs or the food run out (sixteen at most)
- **Over the hills**: somebody who has had no roof for a very long time, and has
  gone hungry, with nothing anywhere that could house them, puts down what is
  the village's, picks up a bundle on a stick, says goodbye and walks out of the
  valley for good. Not dead: there is no cross. Now and then a letter comes back
- **Roof gardens**: a stack that can grow no higher, in a valley with no room for
  another field, gets a flat roof with a parapet, a hatch and a ladder up from
  the top floor, and a bed of sand, five furrows wide: wheat on the roof, which
  the fields a village keeps do not count, and which the ducks find
- **Washing lines**: a flat's window strung to whatever faces it across open air
  (another flat's window, a tree, the next house), and the washing hung out on it
  now and then.
  It is linen, a thing of its own (which you cannot paint: it is theirs). A line
  alight smoulders along like a fuse, and gets in at a shuttered window at the other end (not a
  glass one)

## Run

No build, no dependencies. Open `index.html`, or serve the folder:

    python3 -m http.server

To check it still works, with node (and still no dependencies): `node test/run.js`
plays a few villages headless, with some things forced, and checks them (mines,
galleries, the furnace, lifts, the bell, oil wells, rocket packs, ice). They run side by
side, one process to a core, the longest first (`-j4` for four at a time, `-j1` for one
at a time in the one process; `node test/run.js lift` for the ones with "lift" in their name).
`test/sim.js` is the game without a browser, for writing more. The tests, the harness
and its scenarios run a valley one screen wide (272 columns) unless asked for another
(`sim.load(seed, { gw: 1088 })`, `open({ seed, gw: 1088 })`, `--gw 1088`, or
`PLEASAND_GW=1088`), and a valley one screen wide goes exactly as it did before there
were wide ones. `test/valley.js` checks both, and `docs/wide-valley-spec.md` is how it works.

## Controls

- **Looking along the valley** (it is four screens wide): swipe sideways on a trackpad,
  **Shift-wheel**, or hold **←/→** (**Shift-←/→** a screen at a time, **Home**/**End**
  the ends, **G** back to the village). The strip under the field is the whole valley,
  with everybody on it as dots and a box where you are looking: press it to go there,
  drag it to look along. Drag a stroke, or somebody you are holding, into the left or
  right edge and the view comes along. A news line about somewhere out of view has a
  little arrow on it: click the arrow to go there. A save from before the valley was
  wide loads one screen wide, as it was; NEW makes a wide one
- **Left-drag** paints, **right-drag** erases
- **PEASANT** (V): click to make one and hold it, click one to pick it up,
  drag and let go to throw
- **DUCK** (D): click to make a duck (now and then a mother with ducklings),
  click one to pick it up, drag and let go to throw
- **LIGHTNING** (L): click or hold to strike whatever is under the cursor
- **HEAT** (H) / **COOL** (K): hold to pour heat into things, or draw it out
- **B** (or the BUBBLES button) turns the speech bubbles off and on
- **T** toggles thermal vision; the bar at the bottom shows what is under the
  cursor and how hot it is (and how far along it is boiling, melting or setting)
- **NUKE** (N): one per click. stand back
- **Wheel** changes brush size (over the picker it scrolls the materials instead)
- **Shift-drag** draws a straight line
- **Middle-click / Alt-click** picks up the material under the cursor
- **1–0** pick materials, **F** firework, **E** erase, **↑/↓** cycle, **[ ]** brush size
- **Space / P** pause, **.** single step while paused, **S** / **A** slower / faster (1/4x to 4x;
  a big village in a wide valley may not quite keep up at 4x: the world goes slower, the picture stays smooth)
- **C** clear, **R** new village, **M** mute, **`** fps
- **SAVE** / **LOAD** (top right, or **Ctrl/Cmd-S** / **Ctrl/Cmd-O**) keep the village in a
  `.json` file and bring it back; dropping a save on the game loads it too. A save also
  carries what happened lately (events, deaths, your clicks and keys, any errors, a trail
  of where everybody was, and a few older copies of the world) so a bug can be chased
  from it: `node harness/cli.js save-info FILE`, and see `harness/README.md`

## Files

- `rng.js`: the dice: a seedable `Math.random` (the harness's own), so a save carries them; drawing and sound roll their own
- `audio.js`: the sound department, all synthesised: bangs, babble, screams
- `world.js`: the grid, the materials and their rules, particles, explosions, lightning, the starting village
- `heat.js`: temperature: conduction, convection, melting, boiling, freezing and burning points, and how hot things look
- `peasants.js`: the villagers, what they say, how they die, and how they are drawn
- `paths.js`: how the villagers find their way: A* over everywhere a peasant could stand, using the moves a peasant really makes (steps, drops, leaps, jumps, ladders, ropes, swims)
- `build.js`: what the villagers build (houses, doors, beds, stairs, ladders, bridges, saplings), and living in it
- `ideas.js`: what a well-fed village thinks of, and what comes of it: fields of wheat, lanterns, the bucket brigade, ponds dug and filled, graves, forestry, jetties and fishing, scarecrows, fireworks shows, the statue
- `mines.js`: what is under the sand, and what comes of digging for it: mines and their galleries, coal, iron ore and gold, watchtowers, the furnace and iron, lifts, the bell
- `oil.js`: oil in the rock, the derrick that pumps it, and rocket packs
- `tidy.js`: tidying up: puddles bailed to where water does some good, sand swept off roofs and dug away from doors, piles shovelled up, bushes cut back out of doorways, and the village heap
- `leftovers.js`: what is left over (ash, salt, glass, gunpowder, snow, ice) and what they make of it: picking it up, snowball fights, snowmen, skating, salt on the ice and on the fish, potash, black powder (blasting, fishing with it, fireworks), glass and the glasshouse, and frost on the wheat
- `wonders.js`: the great works, as a kind of thing: two going up at once, the crew, hauling, quarrying, the kiln, concrete and stained glass, tales worth telling, gatherings, the lightning rod, and the WONDERS count
- `monuments.js`: the theatre, the pyramid, and the cathedral with its crypt
- `pleasures.js`: the baths, the hanging gardens, the steam organ, the duck palace and the ice palace
- `heavens.js`: the observatory and its falling stars, the lighthouse, the foundry, and the moon rocket
- `storeys.js`: a crowded valley: flats on top of the houses, their ladders, neighbours, gardyloo and fire, word getting round, going over the hills, roof gardens and washing lines
- `ducks.js`: the ducks
- `save.js`: saving and loading: the village as json (who-is-who kept), plus a journal of what happened, checks, and keyframes for whoever debugs it
- `game.js`: rendering, the picker and HUD, input, and the main loop
- `test/`: the game without a browser (`sim.js`), and some checks that it still works (`run.js`)
- `harness/`: the game without a screen or a browser, built for agents: deterministic runs,
snapshots and rewinds, traces, breakpoints, invariants, a profiler, ascii maps, `why(id)`,
scenario record/replay, and a library, a CLI and an ndjson server. see `harness/README.md`
